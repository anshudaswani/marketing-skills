# Market Context

Give it a company, its product, who it sells to, who it competes with, and whatever customer and market material you can find. It returns a short memo: what is true in the category, what everyone is saying, where the narrative has collapsed into sameness, what is changing, where this company has permission to be different, and a "So what?" that commits to a position.

The output is an argument, not a research report. Two to three pages. If the point needs page five, it is not sharp enough yet. Evidence goes in an appendix file beside the memo.

## Inputs

- The company: name, product, stage, what it claims on its own site.
- Who it sells to: the buyer and the user, if different.
- Named competitors, and the category label the company uses for itself.
- Sources: the company's site and blog, competitor sites, funding announcements, product launch posts, customer reviews (G2, Capterra, app stores, Reddit, forums), analyst and press coverage, job postings, founder interviews, earnings or investor material where it exists.
- If the company's own customer calls or win/loss notes are available, use them. If not, say the memo is built from public information only.

## Procedure

1. Read the company's own words first. Write down, verbatim, how it describes what it does and who it is for. This is the claim you will test.
2. Read five to eight competitors the same way. Collect their one-line claims side by side.
3. Read what customers say, in their language, not the vendor's. Capture exact phrases for pains, triggers, objections, and the things they praise. These are evidence, and later they are hooks.
4. Find what is moving: platform changes, pricing shifts, new entrants, a large player entering, a buyer behavior changing, regulation. Date each one.
5. Lay the competitor claims next to each other and mark where they are interchangeable. Sameness is a finding.
6. Ask where this company could say something true that the others cannot say, or will not. Permission comes from product, from customers, from founders, from timing. Name the source.
7. Write the "So what?" as a single thesis, then two or three implications. Run the genericness check on it. If a competitor could have written it, go back to step 6.
8. Write the memo in the template below. Then write the appendix: every source you opened, with the date and the one fact or quote you took from it.

## Rules

- Public information only, unless the company has given you its own material. Say which in the first line.
- Every claim about the market carries a source in the appendix. No invented numbers.
- Quote customers verbatim or paraphrase without quotation marks. Never stitch two quotes together.
- The thesis has to be falsifiable. "Company X should differentiate through quality" is not a thesis. "AI ad creation is becoming table stakes; the durable position is knowing what deserves to be made" is a thesis, because someone can disagree.
- Short sentences. Plain words. No em dashes. No "leverage", "unlock", "delve", "game-changer", "agentic".
- Two to three pages. Cut background before cutting argument.

## Output

Two files in the same folder: `memo.md` using the template below, and `appendix.md` listing sources and the evidence taken from each.

### Memo template

```
# [Company]: market context
[Date]. Built from public information only. / Built with [company]'s own material.

## The thesis
One paragraph. The claim, stated so it can be argued with.

## What is true
The category as it actually is: who buys, why, what they pay, what has to be true for the product to matter. Three to five short paragraphs. Sourced.

## What everyone is saying
Competitor claims side by side, in their words. One line each.

## Where it has collapsed into sameness
Which claims are interchangeable, and what that costs the category. Name the words that have stopped meaning anything.

## What is changing
Dated shifts that change the answer. Three to five, each with why it matters.

## Where [Company] has permission to be different
Where it could say something true the others cannot. Name the source of the permission: product, customers, founders, timing.

## So what?
The thesis again, then two or three implications: what this means for how the company should talk, what it should stop saying, and the first thing to test. End on the thing a reader would most want to push back on.
```
